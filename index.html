Rules of thumb

  • Signal order is guitar → graphic EQ → analog delay → reverb pedal → amp. EQ shapes the dry tone first; delay repeats that tone; reverb goes last so the echoes sit in the same space.
  • The Deluxe Reverb's reverb and tremolo live only on the Vibrato channel. The Normal channel is dry, which is when a reverb pedal earns its keep. Don't stack the pedal with the amp's reverb.
  • Fender's tone stack cuts mids, so turning Treble and Bass up mostly adds lows and highs around them. A flatter starting point sits around Treble 2–3 and Bass 1–2. At high volume the amp's own breakup brings the mids back, which is why Treble 10 and Bass 1 can sound mid-forward.
  • Set the front-panel volume for the feel you want, then use the rear Output Power switch to set how loud the room gets. Input 1 is full sensitivity; Input 2 is a little quieter and darker (−6 dB).
  • A typical 7-band guitar EQ uses 100 Hz, 200 Hz, 400 Hz, 800 Hz, 1.6 kHz, 3.2 kHz, and 6.4 kHz, each ±15 dB, plus a Level slider with the same range. Prefer cutting a band over boosting it, and keep Level near unity unless you want a solo boost.
  • On an analog delay, Time sets the gap between repeats, Mix is how loud the echo is, and Repeats is how many times it comes back. High Repeats can take off on their own. Many analog delays top out around 300–600 ms, so the longest settings still aren't huge digital echoes.
  • Spring reverb is splashy and a little metallic — the Deluxe's own sound. Plate is smoother and denser, which is the sound the amp can't do. Mix is how much you hear, Tone is brighter clockwise, Decay is how long the tail lasts.
  • These are starting points. Every amp, speaker, guitar, and pedal differs, so finish by ear. A digital Deluxe may also respond a little differently from a vintage one.
